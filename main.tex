\textbf{ABSTRACT}

Local health units frequently rely on manual record-keeping protocols
and fragmented data registries, which can impede routine surveillance
and rapid outbreak tracking. To address these operational constraints,
this study develops a local-first dengue surveillance and early
forecasting platform designed for local health units in Davao City. It
is engineered with a Python-based computational pipeline and a
relational SQLite architecture protected by SQLCipher 256-bit AES
encryption at rest; the platform enforces data security through Werkzeug
password hashing, secure temporary credential provisioning, and
Role-Based Access Control (RBAC).

The system\textquotesingle s core integrates a multi-component
analytical approach: historical non-parametric median (\(P_{50}\)) and
75th percentile (\(P_{75}\)) baselines establish real-time epidemic risk
tiers (Green, Yellow, Red), while Naïve Persistence forecasting serves
as the primary short-term operational predictor for 1-week-ahead case
trajectories, evaluated alongside Linear Regression and an exploratory
Random Forest Regressor and Historical Seasonal Median. The platform
automates warning status notifications and populates an intervention
priority watchlist to track barangay-level case activity (Epidemic
Threshold, Alert Threshold, Normal/Under Alert Threshold). By combining
historical non-parametric centile thresholds with Naïve Persistence
forecasting tailored for sparse, zero-inflated local registries within a
secure, local-first interface, this study presents an epidemiological
risk-tiering and forecasting platform for local health administration.

\textbf{Keywords:} Public Health Informatics, Dengue Surveillance,
Local-First Architecture, SQLCipher At-Rest Encryption, Non-Parametric
Baselines, Statistical Epidemic Thresholds, Naïve Persistence
Forecasting, Role-Based Access Control.

\section{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{CHAPTER 1:
INTRODUCTION}}{CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION}}\label{chapter-1-introduction}

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{1.1 Background of the
Study}}{1.1 Background of the Study}}\label{background-of-the-study}

The management of local epidemiological data typically benefits from
structured digital approaches that bridge aggregate reporting with
operational clarity. Modern health informatics frameworks often
emphasize spatial and temporal disaggregation, wherein surveillance is
examined at granular administrative levels to better observe localized
transmission dynamics. Within the Philippine health ecosystem,
consolidating community-level data into standard operational workflows
involves structured digital protocols aligned with national eHealth
strategies (Sylim et al., 2022). In urbanized administrative divisions
such as Davao City, monitoring health metrics across multiple barangays
involves considerable logistical coordination, highlighting the utility
of localized tracking mechanisms to observe active Dengue transmission
trends.

To establish objective outbreak monitoring protocols informed by
established public health guidelines, public health frameworks emphasize
utilizing historical statistical thresholds to identify unusual case
variations and trigger early responses (World Health Organization, 2009;
Thayer et al., 2025).

Non-parametric quartile thresholding models, specifically employing the
historical median (\(P_{50}\)) and upper quartile (\(P_{75}\)), offer
robust mechanisms for establishing baseline activity while mitigating
the impact of data extremes or outlier values (Ouédraogo et al., 2023;
Rysava et al., 2026). To evaluate prospective case trajectories
alongside statistical baselines, the system incorporates Naïve
Persistence as the primary operational predictor to capture acute
week-to-week persistence, while evaluating Linear Regression and a
comparative Random Forest regressor as exploratory architectural
benchmarks (Zhao et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2025). Integrating these
historical centile thresholds into rule-based alerting logic aligned
with PIDSR parameters provides a structured method for tracking weekly
case trends across local administrative units (Nguyen \& Ming, 2023).

To examine these data processing and security considerations within a
localized context, this study explores a local-first, web-based
prototype designed for Dengue data organization in Davao City
(2023-2026). Developed using a Python processing pipeline and an
encrypted relational SQLite architecture via SQLCipher (Zhang \& Yin,
2021), the prototype adopts a local deployment model to support data
accessibility in low-connectivity settings (Kleppmann et al., 2019) and
foundational storage security.

By structuring data ingestion, barangay-level case aggregation, baseline
estimation, and baseline forecasting outputs, this study aims to examine
how offline-capable architectures equipped with baseline and predictive
modeling can assist local health monitoring workflows.

Problem}}{1.2 Statement of the Problem}}\label{statement-of-the-problem}

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{1.2.1 General
Problem}}{1.2.1 General Problem}}\label{general-problem}

Local health units tracking vector-borne diseases like Dengue often face
operational challenges in rapid data aggregation and localized threshold
generation. While national reporting frameworks exist, routine workflows
at the health district and barangay levels still rely heavily on manual
record entry and fragmented spreadsheet management. This operational
structure introduces processing bottlenecks, potential data entry
errors, and delays in identifying localized epidemiological trends.
Furthermore, standard reporting mechanisms are primarily built for
macro-level surveillance data upload rather than providing frontline
health personnel with automated tools to compute granular historical
baselines (\(P_{50}/P_{75}\)) or run localized near-term predictive
models. At the sub-city level, these delays are further compounded by
data sparsity and small-sample constraints, including low weekly case
counts, zero-inflated morbidity weeks, and irregular reporting
intervals, which degrade the accuracy of conventional statistical
models.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{1.2.2 Specific
Problems}}{1.2.2 Specific Problems}}\label{specific-problems}

  cleaned, normalized, and ingested into a uniform relational database
  schema without data loss or integrity degradation?
\item
  How can an automated analytical pipeline compute historical

  DOH-aligned operational risk tiers (Normal State/Below Alert
  Threshold, Alert Threshold, and Epidemic Threshold) to support early
  epidemiological and outbreak detection?
\item
  How can a multi-model forecasting framework address local data
  sparsity, zero-inflation, and small-sample constraints by utilizing
  Naïve Persistence as the primary operational predictor, benchmarked
  against Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) Linear Regression, historical
  Seasonal Medians, and an exploratory Random Forest regressor, to
  systematically evaluate localized Dengue trajectory predictions and
  populate barangay intervention watchlists?
\item
  How can the application govern multi-user operational access and
  incorporate foundational data protection tools through secure local
  database encryption (SQLCipher), credential hashing, and Role-Based
  Access Control (Admin, BHW, Viewer) with action audit logging in
  resource-constrained local health environments?
\item
  What are the platform's empirical execution characteristics regarding
  technical resource constraints, specifically encrypted database query
  latency, batch ingestion speed, and security authentication hashing
  latency?
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{1.3 Objectives of the
Study}}{1.3 Objectives of the Study}}\label{objectives-of-the-study}

\paragraph{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{1.3.1 General
Objective}}{1.3.1 General Objective}}\label{general-objective}

The main objective of this study is to design, implement, and evaluate
LokalHealth, a local-first, web-based epidemiological surveillance and
forecasting platform tailored for local health units and Barangay Health
Workers operating in resource-constrained environments. The platform is
structured to support point-of-care logging, automated batch template
ingestion, non-parametric historical median (\(P_{50}\)) and 75th
percentile (\(P_{75}\)) risk-tier categorization, and a short-term
forecasting framework centered on Naïve Persistence. To assess
performance under conditions of local data sparsity and small-sample
constraints, the system incorporates a predictive forecasting test bench
comparing Naïve Persistence against Linear Regression, Random Forest,
and seasonal median models, evaluated via time-series cross-validation
errors and pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm-Sidak
correction (\(\alpha = 0.05\)).

\textbf{1.3.2 Specific Objectives}

This research aims to:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  Develop and test an automated batch data ingestion and parsing
  pipeline capable of cleaning, harmonizing, and normalizing disparate
  surveillance templates into a structured relational database schema.
\item
  Implement and evaluate an automated historical non-parametric median
  (\(P_{50}\)) and upper quartile (\(P_{75}\)) baseline calculation
  engine that categorizes active case counts into DOH-aligned risk tiers
  (Normal State/Below Alert Threshold, Alert Threshold, and Epidemic
  Threshold) to support early outbreak detection.
\item
  Implement and test a time-series forecasting framework centered on
  Naïve Persistence as the primary operational predictor under
  conditions of local data sparsity and small sample sizes, supported by
  an empirical model evaluation suite comprising Ordinary Least Squares
  (OLS) Linear Regression, historical Seasonal Medians, and an
  exploratory Random Forest regressor, to populate automated barangay
  intervention watchlists.
\item
  Engineer a secure local-first architecture incorporating data
  protection that features SQLCipher 256-bit AES database encryption,
  Werkzeug password hashing, session control, Role-Based Access Control
  (Admin, BHW, Viewer), and automated audit logging to govern multi-user
  operational access across local health workflows.
\item
  Evaluate the platform's technical efficiency and operational readiness
  through empirical execution time benchmarks and automated test suite
  verification across system workflows.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{1.4 Significance of the
Study}}{1.4 Significance of the Study}}\label{significance-of-the-study}

This study explores practical, methodological, and technical
applications for localized epidemiological tracking across local public
health workflows, health informatics research, and local health
governance. For local health units and primary care personnel, the
platform offers a structured, web-based tool designed to assist City
Health Officers and Barangay Health Workers (BHWs) in organizing
case-level Dengue records across administrative districts in Davao City.
By adopting a local-first software architecture, the prototype aims to
maintain operational availability for record entry and summary
generation during routine network disruptions. Automating the
aggregation of weekly case counts and non-parametric historical
thresholds (\(P_{50}\) median and \(P_{75}\) upper quartile) provides
frontline personnel with an operational watchlist to observe active
trends across barangays without relying on manual spreadsheet
calculations.

Within the domain of public health informatics and software design, this
research evaluates a local-first software prototype engineered for
resource-constrained primary health environments facing small-sample
sizes, data sparsity, and zero-inflated weekly case counts. Rather than
relying on data-hungry machine learning algorithms, the study examines
transparent, low-complexity approaches, specifically evaluating Naïve
Persistence as a short-term operational predictor alongside Ordinary
Least Squares (OLS) Linear Regression, historical Seasonal Medians, and
exploratory Random Forest models as comparative benchmarks under local
data constraints. Furthermore, the implementation combines SQLCipher
256-bit AES database encryption at rest, Werkzeug salted password
hashing, and Role-Based Access Control (Admin, BHW, Viewer) with
automated audit logging, providing an empirical reference for evaluating
localized security and query responsiveness on modest office hardware.

At the administrative level, this study explores how decentralized,
offline digital tools can potentially support local dengue surveillance
workflows. Structuring disaggregated, community-level case records
supports localized early-warning mechanisms and operational planning,
aligning with general public health objectives regarding health
surveillance capacity, resilient primary care infrastructure, and
accessible epidemiological data for local health planning. Specifically,
this framework aligns with the broader objectives of United Nations
Sustainable Development Goal 3 (Target 3.D) by supporting localized
early warning toola, SDG 9 by exploring resilient digital tools for
primary healthcare facilities, and SDG 17 by improving the accessibility
of structured epidemiological data for local health planning.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{1.5 Scope \&
Limitations}}{1.5 Scope \& Limitations}}\label{scope-limitations}

The technical scope of this study comprises the design, implementation,
and evaluation of a web-based, local-first dengue surveillance and
forecasting platform for local health units in Davao City, utilizing
historical surveillance data spanning 2023 through 2026. The software
architecture consists of a Python and Flask analytics processing engine
paired with a relational SQLite database encrypted at rest using
SQLCipher (256-bit AES), supported by Werkzeug cryptographic password
hashing, secure temporary credential provisioning, Role-Based Access
Control (Admin, BHW, Viewer), and automated audit logging.

The dataset consists of city-wide surveillance records compiled by the
City Health Office (CHO), structured across administrative districts and
disaggregated down to the barangay level. Standardized case attributes
include Morbidity Week, Morbidity Month, District, Barangay, Age, Sex,
Clinical Classification, and Case Classification. The system processes
these attributes through three primary analytical functions: descriptive
analytics (generating temporal case distributions and barangay
summaries); predictive analytics (calculating historical non-parametric
\(P_{50}\) median and \(P_{75}\) alert thresholds alongside primary
Naïve Persistence forecasting); and risk-tiering logic executing
rule-based alert triggers (Green for Normal, Yellow for Alert, and Red
for Epidemic).

Methodologically, the scope acknowledges the inherent characteristics of
local epidemiological records, specifically spatial and temporal data
sparsity, zero-inflation across non-outbreak morbidity weeks, small
sample size constraints, and inter-annual archival variations (notably
the lower record volume in the 2025 registry, \(N = 224\)). To prevent
single-year administrative reporting anomalies from distorting threshold
boundaries, predictive evaluations utilize rolling multi-year baseline
pooling (\(N = 3\)) and transparent, low-complexity models.

System evaluation is strictly limited to technical performance metrics
(encrypted query latency, batch template import speed, and password
hashing overhead), automated unit test suites. The study does not
include active clinical trials, longitudinal field deployments, or live
operational testing with health personnel in active clinic settings.
Furthermore, the platform does not incorporate automated clinical
diagnostic algorithms, medical treatment guidance, biological or vector
sensor integrations, live network data sync across devices, or real-time
spatial mapping beyond local record reporting.

\textbf{2.1 Local Surveillance and Barangay Context}

\textbf{2.1.1 Philippine Digital Health and Constraints}

Public health information management in the Philippines exhibits
operational challenges in aligning centralized electronic health
frameworks with primary health facility infrastructure (Sylim et al.,
2022). Data management at the primary care level frequently depends on
manual logs and retrospective batch reporting.

Barangay Health Workers (BHWs) act as primary data collectors within
resource-constrained settings characterized by variable network
availability, hardware constraints, and administrative workloads (De
Mesa et al., 2023). Consequently, routine surveillance records often
encounter transmission delays before reaching local repositories,
impacting the timeliness of local data visibility (Hartigan-Go et al.,
2024).

\textbf{2.1.2 Dengue Surveillance in Davao City}

In urban settings such as Davao City, monitoring vector-borne
transmission across multiple barangays involves distinct data collection
and tracking challenges. Urban density, environmental variations, and
population movement patterns contribute to variable Dengue distribution
trends across districts. While macro-level health statistics provide
broad baseline trends, operational monitoring requires granular,
case-level records.

Under the Philippine Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response
(PIDSR) framework established by the Department of Health (DOH, 2007),
capturing standardized case attributes, specifically Morbidity Week,
Morbidity Month, District, Barangay, Age, Sex, Clinical Classification,
and Case Classification at the point of care establishes the mandatory
data baseline required for disease reporting. Public health informatics
literature confirms that maintaining these granular case attributes
enables local health units to execute localized threshold tracking,
observe spatial-temporal cluster variations across administrative
divisions, and support targeted operational planning (DOH, 2007; Olana
et al., 2025).

\textbf{2.2 Informatics Technologies and Methods}

\textbf{2.2.1 Data Digitization and Normalization}

Transitioning from conventional paper logbooks to structured electronic
registries is an essential prerequisite for local epidemiological
surveillance. Replacing manual logs with structured digital intake
pipelines aims to help primary health units reduce reporting delays
associated with batch reporting, though administrative workflows
continue to introduce variable time lags (Ngusie et al., 2021). Software
architectures must therefore align system usability with structural data
constraints to sustain user engagement among primary healthcare workers
(Whitehead et al., 2023).

To ensure high-integrity intake without redundancy, relational database
models built on Structured Query Language (SQL) enforce necessary
mathematical schema constraints. Applying formal normalization rules to
epidemiological variables prevents record duplication during
high-throughput ingestion (Alderden et al., 2023). Structuring core
attributes including Morbidity Week, Morbidity Month, District,
Barangay, Age, Sex, Clinical Classification, and Case Classification
within a normalized schema facilitates rapid query performance and
long-term longitudinal evaluation without compromising record validity.

\textbf{2.2.2 Local-First Storage and Security}

Deploying web-based systems in settings with variable network access
requires shifting away from always-online cloud designs (Kaboré et al.,
2022). Local-first computing models resolve these network limitations by
processing core database transactions directly on client hardware
(Kleppmann et al., 2019). Operating localized databases with encryption
preserves continuous system availability during infrastructure
disruptions while safeguarding confidential health repositories from
unauthorized exposure (Zhang \& Yin, 2021).

Integrating a local SQLite database engine with Python processing
scripts allows local health facilities to maintain uninterrupted record
entry, local querying, and offline report generation during network
outages (Krishnapur et al., 2026). Furthermore, protecting database
files at rest through standard SQLCipher 256-bit AES encryption provides
a technical safeguard to protect stored data repositories against
unauthorized extraction in the event of physical hardware compromise
(Zhang \& Yin, 2021). Cloud database synchronization operates as an
asynchronous background routine, preserving local system utility
regardless of connectivity state.

\textbf{2.2.3 Analytical Engines and Risk Tiering}

Modern epidemiological surveillance systems utilize structured
computational workflows to convert transactional case records into
actionable local health reporting. To structure the progression from raw
intake data to automated risk tiering, the platform implements a
three-tier analytical engine.

As a primary visualization layer, the platform aggregates patient
attributes into temporal morbidity trend charts, categorical
distributions (by age, sex, and clinical classification), and ranked
barangay-level risk distribution tables. This provides health officers
with immediate temporal visibility and structured spatial breakdowns
across Davao City without relying on complex GIS map rendering (Olana et
al., 2025).

To establish dynamic epidemiological thresholds, the platform implements
non-parametric statistical methods utilizing historical median
(\(P_{50}\)) and upper quartile (\(P_{75}\)) baseline calculations. By
evaluating historical case distributions across a rolling multi-year
archive (\(N = 3\)), this method accounts for seasonal vector dynamics
and identifies statistical surge deviations prior to peak periods while
resisting distortion from extreme data outliers or administrative
reporting gaps (Ouédraogo et al., 2023; Rysava et al., 2026)."

To project short-term case trajectories, the platform incorporates
computational time-series modeling. While tree-based machine learning
approaches (such as Random Forest) capture complex multi-variable
interactions (Zhao et al., 2020; Kuo et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025),
public health informatics literature emphasizes that short-term epidemic
surveillance, specifically at 1-week-ahead operational horizons are
frequently dominated by acute linear autocorrelation and immediate
week-to-week temporal inertia (Al Mobin \& Kamrujjaman, 2023; Shafin,
2026). Furthermore, in local surveillance environments characterized by
small sample sizes, data sparsity, and zero-inflation, high-capacity
non-linear models tend to overfit noise, whereas low-complexity
baselines like Naïve Persistence and Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) Linear
Regression provide stable, transparent, and computationally low-latency
trend estimations (Hyndman \& Athanasopoulos, 2021; Zhao et al., 2020). This justifies the
selection of Naïve Persistence as the primary operational predictor,
paired alongside Linear Regression, historical Seasonal Median
baselines, and an exploratory Random Forest regressor for comparative
error benchmarking.

Lastly, threshold metrics and model outputs are converted into automated
alert statuses using deterministic rule-based logic. By evaluating
active caseloads against historical limits, the platform automatically
generates standardized early warning triggers (Normal State/Below Alert
Threshold, Alert Threshold, and Epidemic Threshold) paired with targeted
intervention protocols to guide local response efforts (Nguyen \& Ming,
2023).

\textbf{2.2.4 Security, Compliance, and Governance}

Integrating security controls into local health architectures is both an
administrative safeguard and a prerequisite for data integrity.
Designing systems in accordance with the data protection principles of
the Philippine Data Privacy Act of 2012 (RA 10173) and the Philippine
Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response guidelines (DOH, 2007)
requires point-of-entry de-identification. This is achieved by excluding
direct identifiers such as patient names, street addresses, and contact
numbers from the relational schema in favor of system-generated internal
keys. System access governance is maintained through Role-Based Access
Control (RBAC), restricting Barangay Health Workers to local entry
functions while reserving system administration and account management
privileges for administrators and City Health Officers (Oktaviana et
al., 2025).

At the application layer, credential protection utilizes salted
key-stretching algorithms via Werkzeug\textquotesingle s native security
utilities (werkzeug.security) to mitigate unauthorized access attempts
(Obiri-Tetteh et al., 2021). Furthermore, local database files and
configuration keys in resource-constrained local health environments are
secured using 256-bit AES encryption via SQLCipher to protect against
physical hardware compromise (Zhang \& Yin, 2021). These security
mechanisms operate within a structured data management framework that
governs records across key operational phases such as data creation,
secure local storage, analytical processing, and longitudinal retention
by ensuring that historical baselines remain intact for multi-year
epidemiological comparison (Oktaviana et al., 2025; Rahul \& Banyal,
2020).

\textbf{2.2.5 Synthesis, Gaps, and Proposed Solution}

Public health informatics literature outlines distinct structural
attributes characterizing contemporary local surveillance environments.
First, a spatial resolution variance frequently occurs because regional
monitoring frameworks prioritize macro-level administrative aggregates,
leaving local health units to handle high-resolution barangay tracking
independently (Sylim et al., 2022; Olana et al., 2025). Second, many
standard operational workflows depend on continuous network
connectivity, presenting logistical challenges in environments subject
to intermittent infrastructure availability (Kaboré et al., 2022).
Third, standard health informatics architectures often separate
transactional data storage from active rprocessing (Ngusie et al., 2021;
Ouédraogo et al., 2023).

To address these architectural parameters, this research implements a
web-based dengue surveillance platform. To manage spatial resolution
alignment, raw multi-year surveillance logs are processed through an
automated schema normalization pipeline that standardizes variable
attributes across reporting periods into core operational parameters
(Morbidity Month, District, Barangay, Age, Sex, Clinical Classification,
Case Classification, and Morbidity Week) for localized temporal and
spatial tracking (DOH, 2007; Alderden et al., 2023). Network constraints
are addressed through a local-first SQLite architecture paired with
Python processing pipelines, allowing offline data management, local
querying, and analytical generation prior to scheduled database
synchronization (Kleppmann et al., 2019; Krishnapur et al., 2026).

To integrate analytics within local workflows, the platform incorporates
a multi-tiered analytical engine: descriptive data aggregations for
temporal and categorical visualization, historical non-parametric median
(\(P_{50}\)) and 75th percentile (\(P_{75}\)) threshold computations for
baseline comparisons (Ouédraogo et al., 2023; Rysava et al., 2026),
Naïve Persistence forecasting as the primary short-term predictor
benchmarked against Linear Regression, historical Seasonal Median, and
Random Forest regression modeling (Zhao et al., 2020; Al Mobin \&
Kamrujjaman, 2023; Kuo et al., 2024; Shafin, 2026), and rule-based risk
tiering logic for operational guidance (Nguyen \& Ming, 2023).
Furthermore, data protection principles, access governance, and
encryption tools are integrated to maintain data confidentiality, and
system integrity. (Oktaviana et al., 2025; Zhang \& Yin, 2021).

\paragraph{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{3.2.1 Data Acquisition and

}{3.2.1 Data Acquisition and Preprocessing }}\label{data-acquisition-and-preprocessing}

The foundational data infrastructure relies on multi-year local health
surveillance records obtained from the City Health Office (CHO) in
Talomo District. Historical records spanning 2023 to 2026 (encompassing
over 16,000 raw patient entries) were acquired. The temporal scope was
intentionally scoped to begin in 2023, as preceding years (2019-2020)
experienced severe healthcare system disruptions due to the COVID-19
pandemic, followed by structural healthcare recovery and transition
phases through 2021 and 2022, making standardized pre-2023 digital line
lists unavailable for consistent baseline modeling.

Dengue is prioritized for surveillance tracking due to its high-volume
temporal profile, making it suitable for baseline threshold modeling.
And Because data collection formats evolved across subsequent annual
reporting cycles that range from detailed clinical columns in 2023
(forATENEO\_DENGUE\_2023.xlsx) to structured layouts in 2024
(forATENEO\_DENGUE\_2024.xlsx) and compact distributions in 2025-2026.

Alongside that, a parsing script was written in Python using the pandas
library. This preprocessing script reads multi-year spreadsheet files,
standardizes varying column headers via string normalization functions
(mapping alternative labels such as Barangay, Current Address Barangay,
and Permanent Address Barangay into a unified schema), and filters out
completely empty rows or spreadsheet artifacts.

\textbf{3.2.2 Exploratory Profiling and Anomaly Identification}

Exploratory profiling of the multi-year local dengue surveillance
registries (2023-2026) obtained from the City Health Office (CHO)
revealed structural data anomalies and reporting discrepancies that
required systematic mitigation prior to baseline modeling. Because
institutional reporting coverage and data archiving protocols vary
across local health jurisdictions and annual reporting cycles, observed
case volumes reflect the specific administrative boundaries and
operational inputs of contributing districts. To evaluate these inputs,
an ingestion corpus was compiled from the district\textquotesingle s
annual spreadsheets, comprising a total of \(N = 16,020\) patient
records (\(N_{2023} = 6,548\) entries in 2023, \(N_{2024} = 7,714\)
entries in 2024, \(N_{2025} = 224\) entries in 2025, and
\(N_{2026} = 1,534\) entries in 2026). Initial exploratory inspection
uncovered substantial data irregularities and temporal inconsistencies
across these cycles.


Specifically, significant temporal and reference errors were identified:
within the 2023 dataset, exactly 6,547 out of 6,548 records (99.98\%)
contained \#REF! spreadsheet reference errors in the Morbidity Month
attribute field, leaving only 1 valid month value. Relying on raw
database queries without preliminary profiling would have resulted in
systematic data loss and distorted epidemiological baselines.
Furthermore, across reporting periods, administrative subdivisions
exhibited spatial and categorical discrepancies with inconsistent text
formatting. In the 2024 registry spanning 18 distinct districts, records
displayed fragmented text entries across current address (182 unique
barangays) and permanent address (195 unique barangays) fields, leading
to divergent district groupings if left unstandardized.
Cross-tabulations between clinical classifications and laboratory
outcomes also indicated reporting variations, where suspect cases
frequently lacked confirmatory diagnostic results during peak
transmission weeks (e.g., 2,319 missing test types and 2,447 missing
laboratory results in 2024; 411 missing results in 2026).

In addition to these structural gaps, the notably lower record volume
observed in the 2025 archive (\(N = 224\)) introduced potential
analytical considerations regarding baseline stability. Whether this
variance stemmed from localized administrative reporting shifts,
temporary disruptions in health unit data extraction workflows, or
genuine transmission fluctuations remained an open observational
variable. To mitigate the risk of baseline skew or distortion caused by
such isolated low-volume intervals, the system\textquotesingle s
baseline architecture employs multi-year data aggregation, pooling
historical trends across multiple reporting cycles to buffer against
single-year anomalies.

\textbf{3.2.2.1 Data Harmonization and Ingestion}

To process variable source files without corrupting the database, the
application\textquotesingle s ingestion pipeline (app.py) implements
specific programmatic cleaning routines. Header harmonization is
executed via the normalize\_upload\_header() function, which strips
encoding characters, converts strings to lowercase, and replaces spaces
and punctuation with underscores to map disparate column titles to
standard database fields. Missing value neutralization is managed by the
coalesce\_text() function to check for empty strings, null values, and
spreadsheet error tokens (such as \#REF!, \#VALUE!, or NaN), assigning
safe fallback labels to preserve record integrity.

Furthermore, integer validation and bounds checking are performed
through parse\_optional\_integer(), verifying that numeric attributes
such as year, morbidity\_month (1-12), morbidity\_week (1-53), and age
(\(\geq 0\)) meet expected numeric constraints before database
insertion. Finally, when case identifiers are missing or use generic
text placeholders, the system automatically generates unique tracking
identifiers (REC-XXXXX) to prevent database collisions.

\textbf{3.2.2.2 Handling of Missing Surveillance Data}

Real-time public health surveillance inherently encounters operational
reporting lags and incomplete reporting windows during active evaluation
periods. Within the application architecture (app.py), missing data
handling is managed through standard database querying logic rather than
complex dynamic imputation models. For historical baseline years (such
as 2023-2025), unrecorded or missing morbidity weeks are evaluated via
standard data retrieval where missing weekly entries default to zero
case counts during aggregation.

Conversely, for the active evaluation year (e.g., 2026), the system
dynamically determines the active tracking window based on the latest
available data present in the database. Specifically,
get\_dashboard\_trend() computes weekly actuals by evaluating reported
records up to the maximum recorded morbidity week
(max(current\_reported\_weeks)), stopping the actuals series after the
latest week present in the data to prevent artificial baseline skew.
Temporal granularity is maintained by aligning weekly counts across
standard ISO morbidity weeks (Weeks 1 through 52) against the rolling
historical baseline window.

Treating unrecorded morbidity weeks as zero cases represents an
operational assumption. While this convention enables continuous
time-series modeling, incomplete historical reporting may still exert
minor localized effects on baseline threshold reliability.

\textbf{3.2.3 Epidemic Thresholds and Risk States}

To track disease transmission intensity and establish an early-warning
risk framework for morbidity week \(w\), the system utilizes dual
non-parametric centiles which are the historical weekly median
(\(P_{50}\)) and the upper quartile (\(P_{75}\)) derived from historical
baseline years (\(y \in BaselineYears\)):

\(Median\ Threshold_{w} = P_{50}(\{ C_{y,w}|y \in BaselineYears\})\)

\(Epidemic\ Threshold_{w} = P_{75}(\{ C_{y,w}|y \in BaselineYears\})\)

While traditional Philippine Integrated Disease Surveillance and
Response (PIDSR) frameworks utilize parametric mean-standard deviation
bounds (\(\mu + 1\sigma\) and \(\mu + 2\sigma\)) for endemic channels
(DOH Administrative Order No. 2007-0036; DOH PIDSR Manual of Procedures,
3rd Edition, 2014), this study adopts non-parametric centile methods
recommended in World Health Organization guidelines (WHO EWARN Handbook,
2019). Non-parametric quartiles are specifically utilized here because
public health environments characterized by extreme seasonal skew,
zero-inflation, and small historical sample windows (\(N = 3\)) violate
the normality assumptions of parametric mean-variance models.

Operational risk classifications are governed by standard non-parametric
quartile boundaries:

\begin{itemize}
\item
  Under Alert Threshold (Green): Case counts remain below the historical
  median (\(CurrentCases < P_{50}(w)\)), indicating normal baseline
  activity.
\item
  Alert Threshold (Yellow):
  \(P_{50}(w) \leq CurrentCases \leq P_{75}(w)\). Case counts breach the
  historical median central tendency and approach the upper quartile,
  functioning as an early-warning signal that prompts local health
  advisories, heightened Barangay Health Worker (BHW) field monitoring,
  and targeted cleanup drives.
\item
  Epidemic Threshold (Red): \(CurrentCases > P_{75}(w)\). Case counts
  exceed the historical 75th centile outbreak boundary, triggering
  vector control, and emergency resource mobilization.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{3.2.3.1 Historical Baseline Pool Structure}

To establish an early-warning risk stratification system without
succumbing to threshold distortion typical of hyper-endemic outlier
years, this study avoids parametric mean-variance methods in favor of
non-parametric centile baselines (\(P_{50}\) and \(P_{75}\)). While the
operational alert structure aligns with national reporting tiers defined
under DOH PIDSR guidelines (AO 2007-0036), the underlying threshold
computation utilizes WHO standard quartile epidemiological methods (WHO
EWARN Handbook, 2019). This non-parametric approach isolates localized
disease surges while preventing extreme seasonal spikes or
zero-reporting drops from distorting normal baseline numbers.

To prevent data leakage and maintain a predictive evaluation design, the
baseline pool utilizes a rolling historical framework derived from a
3-year rolling historical archive (\(N = 3\), spanning 2023-2025 to
evaluate 2026). This 3-year window directly satisfies the minimum
historical data requirement established by WHO EWARN guidelines for
limit-based outbreak detection modeling when longer archives are
unavailable. As detailed in Table 3.1, the pipeline initiates testing in
2024 using a single-year baseline (\(N = 1\)) for preliminary
retrospective validation, scales to a two-year rolling window
(\(N = 2\)) in 2025 to assess inter-annual endemic stability, and
achieves a fully populated three-year archive (\(N = 3\)) for the
primary 2026 prospective test year.

Building upon this temporal framework, model execution for the
predictive forecasting pipeline incorporates strict separation between
historical training corpora and prospective evaluation periods. To
predict weekly trajectory counts without data leakage, input features
are structured using rolling 1-week and 2-week historical case lags
(\(y_{t - 1},y_{t - 2}\)) aligned against exact ISO morbidity week
integers (Weeks 1 through 52) across district and barangay-level
administrative boundaries within Davao City.

\textbf{3.2.3.2 Statistical Behavior of Small Sample Sizes (N=3)}

Given the constraints of multi-year local archives, baseline threshold
calculations operate on a sample size of \(N = 3\) annual observations
per morbidity week. Under this small sample window (\(N = 3\)),
calculating order-statistic centiles relies on discrete ordinal stepping
rather than asymptotic distribution assumptions. For ordered historical
case counts \(C_{(1)} \leq C_{(2)} \leq C_{(3)}\) across baseline years:

Historical Median (\(P_{50}\)): Represents the central ordered
observation, \(P_{50} = C_{(2)}\).

Upper Quartile (\(P_{75}\)): Calculated via standard linear
interpolation between the 2nd and 3rd ordered values:

\(P_{75} = 0.50C_{(2)} + 0.50C_{(3)}\)

This order-statistic median (\(P_{50}\)) helps mitigate the impact of
administrative logging gaps (such as the low-volume 2025 archive,
\(N = 224\)). In an \(N = 3\) baseline pool, a suppressed reporting year
is isolated at the minimum position \(C_{(1)}\). The median baseline
\(P_{50}\) dynamically anchors to \(C_{(2)}\), effectively filtering out
administrative drops and preserving true historical transmission levels.

\textbf{3.2.3.3 \emph{Operational Sensitivity and Risk Framework}}

The non-parametric threshold formulation isolates baseline seasonal
endemicity from anomalous epidemic surges, supported by epidemiological
literature that utilizes upper quartile (P75) baselines for outbreak
detection (Rysava et al., 2026; Thayer et al., 2025). Operational alerts
are deployed through a staged risk framework comprising three distinct
states aligned with DOH PIDSR principles:

\begin{itemize}
\item
  Under Alert Threshold (Green): Case counts remain below the historical
  median (\(CurrentCases < P_{50}(w)\)), indicating normal baseline
  activity.
\item
  Alert Threshold (Yellow): Case counts breach the historical median but
  remain below the upper quartile
  (\(P_{50}(w) \leq CurrentCases \leq P_{75}(w)\)). Functioning as an
  objective early-warning buffer, this tier provides local health
  officers with 1 to 2 weeks of operational lead time to mobilize
  Barangay Health Workers (BHWs) and execute targeted vector reduction
  before full epidemic limits are crossed.
\item
  Epidemic Threshold (Red): Case counts exceed the historical 75th
  percentile (\(CurrentCases > P_{75}(w)\)), signaling a formal outbreak
  breach that requires emergency resource deployment and immediate local
  intervention.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{3.2.4.3 Repository Design and Security Setup}

To organize application components and enforce a functional separation
of concerns, the program's surveillance platform uses a modular
directory layout. The root repository coordinates core system execution
through a centralized entry point (app.py), which integrates the Flask
web framework, SQLCipher database handlers, and baseline forecasting
endpoints. Surrounding this main script are operational directories: an
instance storage directory (instance/) reserved for runtime database
files, a template directory (templates/) housing Jinja2 views for user
authentication and dashboard surveillance interfaces, and a testing
directory containing unit test fixtures to validate data ingestion and
forecasting functions.

To address healthcare data privacy standards and prevent the accidental
tracking or exposure of sensitive patient records, the repository
enforces strict version control exclusion rules via a .gitignore
configuration. Under this protocol, local SQLite and SQLCipher database
files (database.db) reside entirely within the isolated local instance
directory and are systematically barred from source tracking.
Configuration security is further governed through environment variable
templates (.env.example), requiring the explicit injection of the
cryptographic passphrase (DB\_PASSPHRASE) before application runtime.

To ensure cryptographic integrity, startup validation routines within
app.py inspect database headers to detect whether existing files are
encrypted. If an encrypted database is detected without an active
DB\_PASSPHRASE environment variable, execution is blocked and a fatal
security exception is raised to prevent unauthorized data access. Once
authenticated, runtime connection hooks (PRAGMA key) apply 256-bit AES
encryption across all active database transactions via SQLCipher
bindings (sqlcipher3).

System setup across local development or testing environments is managed
through dependency tracking via a pinned package specification file
(requirements.txt). This specifies exact library versions for core
dependencies, including Pandas for epidemiological data manipulation,
Scikit-Learn for machine learning regression pipelines, and SQLCipher
drivers for encrypted storage. Additionally, to streamline initial
setup, the application includes an automated bootstrapping mechanism
(ensure\_primary\_admin\_account) that detects uninitialized databases
and securely seeds a default administrative account upon initial
startup, allowing immediate local-first verification and audit testing.

\textbf{3.2.5 Predictive Modeling and Benchmarking}

\textbf{3.2.5.1 Baseline Selection and Justification}

To empirically evaluate model performance under severe local data
constraints, specifically extreme data sparsity, zero-inflation, and
reporting blackouts (demonstrated by the 2025 Talomo Central archival
gap where \(N = 224\)), The analytical engine establishes a structured
model suite, with each component selected to test specific operational
and mathematical hypotheses:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  Naïve Persistence (\(y_{t} = y_{t - 1}\)): Selected as the primary
  operational baseline to test immediate 1-step-ahead temporal
  continuity. It assumes the subsequent week\textquotesingle s case
  count equals the current week\textquotesingle s observed volume
  without parameter estimation, establishing the minimum threshold of
  predictive accuracy required for short-term outbreak monitoring.
\item
  Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) Linear Regression: Selected as a
  deterministic parametric baseline to evaluate whether capturing
  underlying macro-trends over lag features (\(lag_{1},lag_{2}\)) and
  morbidity week indices improves predictive accuracy over pure
  persistence.
\item
  Exploratory Machine Learning Benchmark (Random Forest Regressor):
  Selected as the representative benchmark for non-linear tree-based
  ensemble architectures due to its widespread adoption in
  epidemiological time-series literature. Under severe local sample
  constraints (\(N = 224\)), non-parametric and parametric ensemble
  variants (e.g., Gradient Boosting, SVR) share identical structural
  susceptibility to high variance and overfitting on sparse lag
  matrices. Thus, Random Forest serves as a controlled proxy to evaluate
  whether non-linear feature interaction modeling offers statistical
  advantages over closed-form deterministic baselines.
\item
  Historical Seasonal Median Baseline: Selected as a retrospective
  seasonal baseline capturing long-term historical median weekly case
  counts from the baseline pool to account for macro-seasonality.
\end{enumerate}

For retrospective surveillance, the historical 75th percentile
(\(P_{75}\)) method is utilized as the non-parametric statistical
baseline. Grounded in epidemiological surveillance principles (Chapter
2, Section 2.3.3), this baseline establishes objective historical case
thresholds to translate active weekly case counts into DOH-aligned
operational risk tiers, categorizing local conditions into Normal State
(Below Alert Threshold), Alert Threshold, and Epidemic Threshold to
support early outbreak detection and alert triggers (Rysava et al.,
2026).

\textbf{3.2.5.2 Evaluation Metrics and Statistical Validation}

Model performance and operational utility are assessed using an
integrated evaluation framework that strictly separates continuous
forecasting error evaluation from threshold classification and
risk-tiering validation.

For forecasting error evaluation, the system relies on the Root Mean
Squared Error (RMSE) to heavily penalize larger outbreaks or missed
surge peaks, alongside the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) to quantify the
average magnitude of absolute forecast errors. To rigorously validate
model performance across the defined baseline hierarchy, pairwise
difference tests are applied to the forecasting residuals. Because local
epidemiological error distributions are inherently skewed,
zero-inflated, and prone to heavy tails, the non-parametric Wilcoxon
signed-rank test is utilized for pairwise model comparisons. Family-wise
error rates across multiple model comparisons are controlled using
Holm-Sidak correction, enforcing a strict significance threshold of
\(\alpha = 0.05\).

Because operational thresholds function as epidemiological
classification boundaries rather than continuous point predictions,
their performance is evaluated separately using a multi-class confusion
matrix, surveillance metrics, and lead-time analysis framework mapped
against dual historical baselines:

\(Median\ Threshold_{w} = P_{50}(\{ C_{y,w}|y \in BaselineYears\})\) and

\(Epidemic\ Threshold_{w} = P_{75}(\{ C_{y,w}|y \in BaselineYears\})\):

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  Green Tier (\textless P50) Evaluation: Measures baseline specificity
  and false-negative avoidance. This verifies how accurately the system
  maintains routine monitoring states during periods of normal endemic
  activity, ensuring non-surge weeks do not trigger premature alarms or
  deplete local vector-control resources.
\item
  Yellow Tier (\(P_{50} \leq Cases \leq P_{75}\)) Evaluation: Measures
  early-warning sensitivity (true positive rate) and operational lead
  time. This quantifies the advance warning buffer, specifically the
  number of morbidity weeks local health officers gain when case counts
  breach the central tendency (\(P_{50}\)) before reaching the upper
  quartile outbreak ceiling (\(P_{75}\)).
\item
  Red Tier (\(> P_{75}\)) Evaluation: Measures outbreak detection
  specificity, surge recall, and precision, verifying how the upper
  quartile boundary isolates true historical epidemic spikes from
  routine seasonal variation while minimizing false-alert rates.
\item
  Risk-Tiering and Simulated Scenario Walkthroughs: Evaluates how
  retrospective centile thresholds interact with short-term predictive
  trends (Naïve Persistence) to generate and rank the dynamic
  Intervention Priority Watchlist across high-risk barangays. To
  validate real-world operational utility, the system is subjected to
  simulated qualitative risk-tiering scenarios demonstrating how health
  officers interpret alert tiers and execute targeted vector-control
  protocols prior to full epidemic thresholds being crossed.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{4.2.2 Historical Baseline and Gaps Evaluation}

A critical methodological challenge encountered during exploratory
profiling was the significant inter-annual variance observed in the 2025
archive (\(N = 224\)). In traditional parametric surveillance systems,
abrupt volume contractions of this magnitude introduce severe
distortion, skewing mean-variance calculations and triggering false
epidemic alarms or artificial suppression.

To evaluate whether this variance compromised baseline stability, the
system\textquotesingle s architecture implemented a rolling 3-year
historical baseline window (\(N = 3\), pooling 2023-2025) coupled with
non-parametric order-statistic centiles. Under an \(N = 3\) baseline
pool for any given morbidity week \(w\), ordered historical case counts
across baseline years are denoted as
\(C_{(1)}\  \leq C_{(2)}\  \leq C_{(3)}\). The historical median
(\(P_{50}\)) is mathematically defined as the central ordered
observation:

\(P_{50} = C_{(2)}\)

This order-statistic formulation provides robust mathematical resilience
against single-year administrative anomalies. Specifically, when a
suppressed reporting year (such as the 2025 archive with \(N = 224\))
occurs, it is systematically isolated at the minimum position
\(C_{(1)}\). Because the non-parametric median baseline \(P_{50}\)
dynamically anchors to the central ordered value \(C_{(2)}\) rather than
the arithmetic mean, the system effectively filters out single-year
administrative drops without requiring complex artificial imputation.
This non-parametric order-statistic formulation helps reduce sensitivity
to isolated low-reporting years (such as the 2025 archive gap) without
requiring dynamic artificial imputation.

competitive held-out test performance (\(RMSE = 18.96\),
\(MAE = 12.97\)). Because RMSE heavily penalizes larger residual
deviations while MAE measures average absolute error, Naïve
Persistence\textquotesingle s lower scores indicate that short-term
week-to-week tracking limits large outlier projection errors during
low-volume intervals. Linear Regression achieved a comparable held-out
test RMSE of 18.64 and an MAE of 13.30, performing on par with
persistence. Conversely, the Random Forest model exhibited elevated
error metrics (\(CVRMSE = 27.97\), \(TestRMSE = 28.29\)), demonstrating
that complex non-linear models tend to overfit noise under small sample
size constraints (\(N = 224\)). Similarly, the Seasonal Median model
yielded substantially higher errors (\(CVRMSE = 76.84\),
\(TestRMSE = 54.97\)), illustrating that static multi-year historical
medians fail to capture acute week-to-week transmission shifts during
active outbreaks.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{4.3.4 Operational Trade-off
Analysis}}{4.3.4 Operational Trade-off Analysis}}\label{operational-trade-off-analysis}

\subsection{}\label{section-11}

The empirical findings reconcile a core operational reality in local
health informatics: short-term weekly disease trajectories exhibit
strong temporal inertia and autocorrelation. In resource-constrained
local health units across Davao City, which are characterized by sparse
reporting intervals and zero-inflated weekly case distributions, complex
machine learning models fail because they attempt to learn non-linear
feature interactions from insufficient data points.

Conversely, Naïve Persistence dominates immediate point-estimates
because disease transmission week-to-week exhibits high autocorrelation,
meaning cases reported in week \(t\) strongly predict week \(t + 1\). By
coupling Naïve Persistence as the primary operational predictor with
non-parametric historical centile baselines (\(P_{50}/P_{75}\)), the
system maintains low computational latency, deterministic calculations,
and practical resilience against minor reporting irregularities to
assist local health monitoring workflows conditionally.

issuing local health advisories, and scheduling preemptive vector
control protocols before full epidemic limits are reached, thereby
supporting proactive local response planning.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{4.5.1 Watchlist Population
Mechanics}}{4.5.1 Watchlist Population Mechanics}}\label{watchlist-population-mechanics}

To translate complex statistical outputs into immediate operational
clarity for primary health workers, the platform automatically generates
a dynamically sorted Intervention Priority Watchlist (dashboard.html).

The backend analytical engine (app.py) evaluates active weekly case
counts against dual non-parametric baselines and short-term forecast
trajectories, assigning each barangay a multi-variable risk profile
based on three hierarchical criteria:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  Current Risk State Tiering: Barangays are primarily categorized into
  distinct operational tiers. The Red Tier (High Risk / Outbreak State)
  applies when active cases exceed the upper quartile baseline
  (\(Cases_{t} > P_{75}\)), assigning the highest priority rank
  (Priority 1). The Yellow Tier (Alert State / Early Warning) triggers
  when active cases breach the median baseline but remain below the
  upper quartile (\(P_{50} \leq Cases_{t} \leq P_{75}\)), assigned a
  secondary priority rank (Priority 2). Finally, the Green Tier (Normal
  State) designates routine monitoring when cases remain below the
  median baseline (\(Cases_{t} < P_{50}\), Priority 3).
\item
  Observed Trend Indicator: Within each risk tier, barangays are
  sub-sorted using an observed week-to-week trend indicator
  (\(y_{t} - y_{t - 1}\)). A positive observed difference flags rising
  weekly momentum, whereas neutral or negative differences designate
  stable or declining trends. Because Naïve Persistence projects the
  immediate prior week\textquotesingle s count (\(y_{t + 1} = y_{t}\)),
  trajectory directionality is calculated from actual observed
  week-to-week changes rather than a multi-step predictive slope.
\item
  Absolute Case Volume: Any remaining ties in tier status and trajectory
  are broken by sorting barangays in descending order of absolute weekly
  case counts.
\end{enumerate}

This rule-based sorting mechanism ensures that frontline Barangay Health
Workers (BHWs) and City Health Officers are immediately presented with
high-risk and rapidly escalating barangays upon logging in, eliminating
manual spreadsheet filtering.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{4.7 Results
Summary}}{4.7 Results Summary}}\label{results-summary}

This chapter presented the empirical findings, validation metrics, and
performance evaluations of the local-first dengue surveillance and
risk-tiering platform. Using multi-year historical registries spanning
2023 through 2026 for Talomo District (\(N = 16,020\) total records;
observational matrix of \(N = 7,798\) active barangay-weeks), the
evaluation verified the operational characteristics of the platform's
multi-tiered analytical engine.

The time-series forecasting evaluation compared Naïve Persistence
against Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) Linear Regression, a Random Forest
regressor, and historical Seasonal Medians. Under conditions of data
sparsity and zero-inflation, continuous error metrics and Wilcoxon
signed-rank tests (\(p < 0.05\) with Holm-Sidak corrections) indicated
that Naïve Persistence and OLS Linear Regression yielded lower error
distributions than complex non-linear and static seasonal baselines.
These results reflect the high temporal autocorrelation inherent in
short-term weekly disease incidence.

The non-parametric threshold engine, utilizing historical median
(\(P_{50}\)) and upper quartile (\(P_{75}\)) centiles derived from a
rolling 3-year baseline pool (\(N = 3\)), successfully categorized
historical case registries into standardized DOH PIDSR risk tiers
(Green, Yellow, and Red). Multi-class classification performance
demonstrated reliable specificity and recall across operational
boundaries, while historical lead-time analysis across 127 outbreak
episodes yielded an average operational advance warning buffer of 1.80
morbidity weeks between the Yellow alert and Red epidemic thresholds.

Finally, system-level technical benchmarking and automated security
audits confirmed that the application operates within acceptable
performance and access-control constraints. Encrypted database queries
via SQLCipher (256-bit AES), batch spreadsheet ingestion routines, and
Werkzeug password hashing met Human-Computer Interaction (HCI)
responsiveness targets (\textless{} 200 ms latency), resident set size
(RSS) memory utilization remained below the 160 MB operational ceiling,
and automated security assertions verified both storage integrity and
RBAC boundary enforcement.

\textbf{CHAPTER 5: Conclusions and Recommendations}

\textbf{5.1 Conclusions}

This study evaluated LokalHealth, a local-first epidemiological
surveillance and forecasting prototype designed for local health units
and Barangay Health Workers (BHWs) in primary care environments such as
Davao City. By integrating automated schema normalization,
non-parametric historical centile baselines (\(P_{50}\) and \(P_{75}\)),
short-term Naïve Persistence forecasting, and local database encryption,
the platform addresses operational characteristics associated with
manual record-keeping and fragmented data spreadsheets.

Revisiting the general problem and the specific objectives established
in Chapter 1, empirical testing confirmed the technical implementation
of each system component:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  Regarding the first objective on data ingestion and normalization, the
  application incorporated a batch parsing pipeline (app.py) for
  cleaning, harmonizing, and normalizing legacy surveillance templates
  in .xlsx and .csv formats. The pipeline processed a multi-year corpus
  of 16,020 patient records spanning 2023 through 2026. Programmatic
  routines, specifically header normalization
  (normalize\_upload\_header()), null value neutralization
  (coalesce\_text()), and integer bounds checking
  (parse\_optional\_integer()), addressed structural reference issues,
  including \#REF! anomalies in the 2023 dataset, while standardizing
  textual entries across administrative districts. Technical execution
  benchmarks measured a batch template ingestion speed of 35.33 ms.
\item
  Regarding the second objective on baseline calculation and risk
  tiering, the platform implemented a historical median (\(P_{50}\)) and
  upper quartile (\(P_{75}\)) calculation engine aligned with World
  Health Organization (WHO) EWARN standards and Department of Health
  (DOH) PIDSR guidelines. Operating on a rolling 3-year historical
  baseline pool (\(N = 3\), 2023--2025), the order-statistic formulation
  (\(P_{50} = C_{(2)}\)) categorized active weekly case counts across an
  observational matrix of 7,798 active barangay-weeks into operational
  risk tiers: Green (Normal State / Below Alert Threshold,
  \(Cases < P_{50}\)), Yellow (Alert Threshold,
  \(P_{50} \leq Cases \leq P_{75}\)), and Red (Epidemic Threshold,
  \(Cases > P_{75}\)). Multi-class classification evaluation yielded
  100.00\% recall for the Green tier, 88.35\% early-warning sensitivity
  for the Yellow tier, and 92.10\% specificity with 84.20\% surge recall
  for the Red tier. Historical lead-time analysis across 127 recorded
  outbreak episodes indicated an average advance warning buffer of 1.80
  morbidity weeks between the median and upper quartile thresholds.
\item
  Regarding the third objective on time-series forecasting and priority
  watchlists, the platform evaluated a short-term forecasting framework
  centered on Naïve Persistence (\(y_{t + 1} = y_{t}\)) as the primary
  operational baseline, evaluated alongside Ordinary Least Squares (OLS)
  Linear Regression, historical Seasonal Medians, and an exploratory
  Random Forest regressor under local data sparsity (\(N = 224\)).
  Continuous forecasting error evaluation and non-parametric Wilcoxon
  signed-rank tests with Holm--Šidák correction (\(\alpha = 0.05\))
  demonstrated that Naïve Persistence and OLS Linear Regression produced
  lower error distributions (\(p_{adj} < 0.001\)) than the non-linear or
  static seasonal models evaluated within this dataset. These results
  reflect the temporal autocorrelation observed in short-term weekly
  disease incidence within sparse registries. Using these forecasting
  outputs and historical centile thresholds, the system automated the
  population and sorting of an Intervention Priority Watchlist to order
  high-risk and escalating barangays.
\item
  Regarding the fourth objective on security, encryption, and access
  control, the system incorporated SQLCipher 256-bit AES database
  encryption at rest, Werkzeug salted password hashing, session
  controls, Role-Based Access Control (Admin, BHW, Viewer), and
  automated audit logging. Security testing (test\_security.py) verified
  policy enforcement across the RBAC authorization matrix, isolating
  administrative endpoints and record modification functions.
  File-header inspection verified custom SQLCipher binary signatures
  (b\textquotesingle SQLCIPHER\_AES\_25\textquotesingle) on local SQLite
  database files, while Werkzeug key-stretching executed salted password
  hashing in 94.57 ms.
\item
  Regarding the fifth objective on technical efficiency and resource
  constraints, application performance was evaluated through execution
  benchmarks and automated test suite verification across core
  workflows. Performance benchmarks (test\_benchmarks.py) recorded
  encrypted database query latency averaging 123.44 ms and
  authentication hashing completing in 92.11 ms. System resource
  profiling indicated that Resident Set Size (RSS) memory utilization
  remained bounded between 74.57 MB and 75.59 MB, below the 160.00 MB
  operational target established for local hardware evaluation.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{5.2 Future Work}

This section outlines the methodological scope and technical limitations
of the study, followed by potential development pathways for subsequent
research.

\textbf{5.2.1 Limitations}

The scope and technical boundaries of this study are defined by several
specific parameters:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  First, the system does not incorporate real-time environmental or
  meteorological covariates. The platform\textquotesingle s analytical
  engine relies on historical epidemiological case counts and rolling
  lag features (\(lag_{1},lag_{2}\)). It does not ingest real-time
  meteorological variables (such as precipitation depth, ambient
  temperature, or relative humidity) or vector entomological
  surveillance indicators (such as Breteau indices or ovitrap positivity
  rates).
\item
  Second, the system operates as a single-node local deployment. While
  the SQLite database encrypted via SQLCipher supports local record
  storage and offline access on a single device, the prototype does not
  implement active peer-to-peer (P2P) multi-device synchronization
  protocols or Conflict-Free Replicated Data Types (CRDTs) for
  decentralized mesh syncing across multiple disconnected facilities.
\item
  Third, the evaluation was conducted without longitudinal field trials
  involving operational end-users. System testing was restricted to
  automated software benchmark suites (test\_benchmarks.py,
  test\_security.py), historical cross-validation, and multi-year
  back-testing against 16,020 archived records. The study did not
  perform live clinical deployments or formal usability trials with
  Barangay Health Workers in active health center environments.
\item
  Fourth, the platform maintains a static single-disease scope. The
  ingestion pipeline and analytical thresholds are configured for
  vector-borne transmission using dengue-specific DOH PIDSR guidelines.
  The relational schema and baseline logic are not dynamically
  parameterized to simultaneously model multi-pathogen syndromic
  surveillance.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{5.2.2 Extensions}

Based on the limitations identified above, future researchers can
consider the following technical extensions:

First, future studies could evaluate the integration of exogenous
meteorological covariates into the forecasting pipeline. Developers may
extend the Python backend (app.py) and forecasting experimentation
module (forecasting\_experiments.ipynb) to ingest weather API feeds or
regional PAGASA meteorological logs capturing precipitation depth and
mean temperature. The feature engineering matrix could incorporate
multi-week lagged weather variables (\(t - 2\) through \(t - 4\)) to
test whether augmenting Naïve Persistence and Linear Regression with
climate features alters out-of-sample RMSE and MAE across test folds
using paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (\(\alpha = 0.05\)).

Second, future research could explore asynchronous peer-to-peer (P2P)
synchronization protocols. The local-first storage architecture could be
expanded from a single-node SQLite model to a decentralized
synchronization framework by testing a transport layer utilizing
WebSockets or Wi-Fi Direct socket communication paired with
cryptographic event logs. Researchers could evaluate data
synchronization integrity by simulating network partitions between
independent offline node instances, measuring conflict resolution
latency, payload encryption overhead, and data merge consistency upon
network reconnection.

Third, subsequent work could involve formal longitudinal usability
evaluations and field trials. Researchers could collaborate with local
health offices and Barangay Health Stations to deploy the web platform
on mobile field tablets for a multi-month pilot study. This evaluation
could assess task completion times for batch ingestion and watchlist
navigation, system adoption metrics, and user feedback via standardized
System Usability Scale (SUS) questionnaires.

Fourth, future architectural efforts could generalize the platform for
multi-pathogen syndromic surveillance. The relational database schema
and ingestion parsing pipeline (normalize\_upload\_header()) could be
refactored to support dynamic pathogen configurations beyond dengue.
Incorporating modular disease classification tables would allow
adjustable baseline lookback windows and adaptable WHO EWARN centile
calculation rules (such as adjusting between \(P_{75}\) and \(P_{90}\)
outbreak thresholds). Subsequent work could validate this generalized
schema by ingesting multi-disease historical morbidity logs and
evaluating whether the risk-tiering engine partitions distinct alert
thresholds across separate pathogen categories without schema conflicts.

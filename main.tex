\begin{quote}
\textbf{ABSTRACT}
\end{quote}

The governance of municipal epidemiological intelligence is increasingly
characterized by a transition toward high-resolution, decentralized
digital architectures. Within the Philippine health ecosystem, local
units navigate significant operational hurdles stemming from fragmented
legacy registries, manual record-keeping protocols, and structural data
unstandardization, which collectively impede proactive surveillance and
rapid outbreak detection. To mitigate these processing bottlenecks, this
investigation develops a Web-Based Decision Support System leveraging a
Three-Tier Analytics Framework optimized for localized Dengue monitoring
in Davao City (2023-2026). Engineered with a Python-based computational
pipeline and a relational SQLite architecture, the platform utilizes a
local-first storage model and Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) to
maintain data integrity in resource-limited edge deployments while
ensuring compliance with the Data Privacy Act (RA 10173).

The system\textquotesingle s processing core executes across three
integrated layers: Tier 1 Descriptive Analytics normalizes granular
attributes to visualize spatial heatmaps and temporal curves by
morbidity week and barangay; Tier 2 Predictive Analytics applies 75th
percentile moving regression and historical epidemic thresholds to
establish statistical surge trajectories; and Tier 3 Prescriptive
Analytics automates rule-based early warning alert triggers (Green,
Yellow, Red) to operationalize targeted vector interventions and
resource management. By shifting municipal surveillance from
retrospective reporting to proactive decision intelligence, the research
provides a scalable digital framework for high-resolution health
tracking.

Keywords: Public Health Informatics, Dengue Surveillance, Three-Tier
Analytics Framework, Early Warning Systems, Statistical Epidemic
Thresholds, Local-First Architecture, Python, SQLite, Data Privacy Act
(RA 10173), Role-Based Access Control

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{1.1 Background of the
Study}}{1.1 Background of the Study}}\label{background-of-the-study}

The governance of municipal epidemiological data is increasingly defined
by a shift toward high-resolution, localized digital architectures.
Modern health informatics paradigms emphasize spatial and temporal
disaggregation, wherein surveillance is executed at granular
administrative levels to capture localized transmission dynamics. Within
the Philippine digital health ecosystem, consolidating community-level
surveillance into national frameworks depends on implementing
interoperable digital standards (Sylim et al., 2022). In highly
urbanized centers such as Davao City, managing 182 distinct barangays
involves significant operational complexity, necessitating a transition
from aggregate monthly reporting to micro-level tracking to monitor
active Dengue transmission (Otero et al., 2022).

At the primary care level, localized health data management frequently
relies on unstandardized spreadsheets or manual registers, creating
structural normalization and synchronization bottlenecks (Kaboré et al.,
2022). Recent evaluations of Philippine Dengue surveillance indicate
that traditional passive reporting utilizing multi-year moving averages
fails to detect seasonal surges, as historical baselines are distorted
by epidemiological anomalies (Eligado et al., 2024). Furthermore,
provincial spatial analyses demonstrate that Dengue resurgence exhibits
distinct geographic clustering, reinforcing the requirement for
localized time-series analytical models (Olana et al., 2025).

To bridge predictive analytics with health interventions, statistical
epidemic thresholds are required to trigger early warning mechanisms.
Non-parametric percentile thresholding models provide higher sensitivity
for detecting abnormal case surges compared to standard mean-based
models, which frequently generate inflated baselines during epidemic
cycles (Ouédraogo et al., 2023). Implementing dynamic percentile
thresholds on routine surveillance data balances sensitivity and
specificity, enabling real-time detection without excessive false alarms
(Thayer et al., 2025). When integrated with rule-based decision support
logic, these statistical thresholds provide personnel with actionable
protocols, reducing cognitive load during high-caseload periods (Nguyen
\& Ming, 2023; Papadopoulos et al., 2022).

To address these data processing constraints, this study presents a
Web-Based Public Health Decision Support System incorporating a
Three-Tier Analytics Framework tailored for Dengue surveillance in Davao
City (2023-2026). Developed using a Python processing pipeline and a
relational SQLite architecture, the system operates on a local-first
deployment model to maintain data availability in low-connectivity
environments (Paluyo et al., 2022) while adhering to the Philippine Data
Privacy Act of 2012 (RA 10173). By operationalizing descriptive
spatial-temporal modeling, predictive regression thresholds, and
prescriptive alert triggers, the system transitions municipal
surveillance from retrospective reporting to decision-support
intelligence.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{1.5 Scope and
Limitations}}{1.5 Scope and Limitations}}\label{scope-and-limitations}

The technical scope of this study comprises the development and
evaluation of a Web-Based Public Health Decision Support System
incorporating a Three-Tier Analytics Framework for localized Dengue
surveillance in Davao City, utilizing case data from 2023 to 2026. The
software architecture consists of a Python analytics processing engine
and a relational SQLite database configured for local-first operations
to support local data query execution during network disconnection
(Paluyo et al., 2022).

The dataset is defined by eight standardized Dengue case variables:
Morbidity Week, Morbidity Month, District, Barangay, Age, Sex, Clinical
Classification, and Case Classification. The system processes these
attributes through three analytical components: Tier 1 Descriptive
analytics generating spatial heatmaps and temporal distribution curves;
Tier 2 Predictive analytics calculating 75th percentile moving
regression and historical deviation thresholds (Ouédraogo et al., 2023;
Thayer et al., 2025); and Tier 3 Prescriptive analytics executing
rule-based alert triggers (Green, Yellow, and Red). Data management
complies with the Philippine Data Privacy Act of 2012 (RA 10173) by
excluding personally identifiable information, including patient names,
street addresses, and contact details. Access governance is structured
through Role-Based Access Control (RBAC), defining operational
privileges for Barangay Health Workers (BHWs) and Municipal Health
Officers.

System evaluation is limited to software performance metrics,
specifically database query latency (in milliseconds), offline-to-online
data synchronization consistency, and user interface usability measured
through standard System Usability Scale (SUS) benchmarks. The system
does not include automated diagnostic algorithms, clinical decision
support for patient treatment, or biological sensor integration. Offline
database capabilities are limited to local record entry, querying, and
reporting, requiring active network connectivity solely for central
administrative database synchronization. Spatial and temporal analytical
functions process municipal-level records across Davao City, while user
usability evaluations are conducted with health personnel operating
within the Talomo District (Otero et al., 2022).

\textbf{2.1 Municipal Epidemiological Surveillance and Barangay
Realities}

\textbf{2.1.1 The Philippine Digital Health Landscape and Local
Surveillance Constraints}

Public health information management in the Philippines involves an
operational gap between centralized electronic health strategies and
primary health facility infrastructure. While national policies mandate
interoperable digital health systems (Sylim et al., 2022), data
management at the primary care level relies predominantly on manual
logbooks and retrospective batch reporting. Barangay Health Workers
(BHWs) serve as primary points of data collection but operate within
resource-constrained environments affected by variable network
connectivity, hardware limitations, and administrative overhead (De Mesa
et al., 2023). Consequently, routine surveillance records experience
transmission lags before reaching municipal databases, delaying
localized outbreak detection at the community level (Hartigan-Go et al.,
2024).

\textbf{2.1.2 Dengue Epidemiology and Granular Surveillance in Davao
City}

In municipal centers such as Davao City, tracking vector-borne
transmission across 182 distinct barangays introduces spatial and
temporal monitoring challenges. Urban density, micro-climatic
variations, and population movements contribute to localized Dengue
surge trajectories that are non-uniform across districts.

While broad environmental surveillance, including municipal wastewater
monitoring, provides population-level estimates (Otero et al., 2022),
operational decision support requires case-level epidemiological
records. Current public health informatics research indicates that
capturing standardized case attributes (Morbidity Week, Morbidity Month,
District, Barangay, Age, Sex, Clinical Classification, and Case
Classification) at the point of care provides the empirical foundation
necessary to execute localized threshold calculations and vector
intervention planning (Olana et al., 2025; Paluyo et al., 2022).

\textbf{2.2 Technologies and Methodologies for Dengue Health
Informatics}

\textbf{2.2.1 Primary Data Digitization and Schema Normalization}

The transition from manual paper logbooks to structured digital
registries represents a foundational requirement for municipal
epidemiological monitoring. Replacing paper-based records with
structured digital ingestion enables health units to eliminate
transmission delays inherent in aggregate reporting (Thakur et al.,
2023). However, digital health deployments in resource-limited
environments face operational friction if user workflows introduce
excessive administrative overhead (Ngusie et al., 2021).

System architectures must therefore balance user-centered design with
structural integrity to maintain consistent data entry compliance among
primary personnel (Whitehead et al., 2023).

To ensure high-integrity data ingestion without structural redundancy,
Relational Database Management Systems (RDBMS) utilizing Structured
Query Language (SQL) provide necessary mathematical integrity
constraints (Antas et al., 2022). Applying relational normalization
rules to epidemiological variables prevents data anomalies during
high-frequency ingestion (Alderden et al., 2023; Izonin et al., 2022).
Linking case-specific parameters, including Morbidity Week, Morbidity
Month, District, Barangay, Age, Sex, Clinical Classification, and Case
Classification, within a normalized schema enables efficient querying
and long-term longitudinal aggregation without record corruption.

\textbf{2.2.2 Edge Execution, Local-First Storage, and Processing
Pipelines}

Deploying web-based decision support systems in settings with unstable
network infrastructure requires an architectural shift away from
traditional, always-online cloud paradigms (Kaboré et al., 2022).
Local-first and edge computing paradigms address these connectivity
constraints by executing core database transactions and processing
routines directly on local client hardware (Jazaeri et al., 2023; Paluyo
et al., 2022).

Integrating a local-first SQLite database engine with Python processing
pipelines allows primary health units to maintain continuous record
creation, query execution, and local report generation during network
disruptions (Krishnapur et al., 2026). Centralized cloud database
synchronization is handled as an asynchronous background routine,
preserving local operational continuity regardless of external network
state.

\textbf{2.2.3 Computational Analytics Paradigms for Disease
Surveillance}

Modern epidemiological decision support systems rely on multi-tiered
analytical processing to transform raw transactional records into health
intelligence. To establish a structured progression from raw case data
to actionable decision support, the system incorporates a Three-Tier
Analytics Framework:

\begin{itemize}
\item
  Tier 1 (Descriptive Analytics): Serves as the foundational
  visualization layer, aggregating raw case attributes into spatial
  heatmaps and temporal distribution curves across barangay and district
  boundaries. This provides public health officers with real-time
  micro-level spatial tracking and seasonal trend analysis (Olana et
  al., 2025).
\item
  Tier 2 (Predictive Analytics): Incorporates non-parametric statistical
  methods, specifically a 75th percentile moving regression model
  combined with historical baseline comparisons to establish dynamic
  epidemic thresholds. By evaluating multi-year historical case
  distributions (2023-2026), this tier accounts for seasonal vector
  transmission cycles and identifies statistically significant case
  deviations before peak transmission periods (Ouédraogo et al., 2023;
  Thayer et al., 2025).
\item
  Tier 3 (Prescriptive Analytics): Translates statistical threshold
  outputs into operational decision support through deterministic,
  rule-based decision logic. By evaluating real-time case counts against
  Tier 2 baseline thresholds, the engine automatically triggers
  standardized, color-coded alert notifications (Green for Normal,
  Yellow for Warning, and Red for Epidemic Surge) to guide municipal
  officers in deploying targeted vector control interventions (Nguyen \&
  Ming, 2023).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{2.2.4 Health Data Security, Regulatory Compliance, and
Governance}

Integrating security controls into municipal health architectures is
both a regulatory requirement and a prerequisite for data integrity.
Compliance with the Philippine Data Privacy Act of 2012 (RA 10173)
requires point-of-entry de-identification, which is achieved by
excluding direct identifiers such as patient names, street addresses,
and contact numbers from the relational schema in favor of
system-generated internal keys. System access governance is maintained
through Role-Based Access Control (RBAC), restricting Barangay Health
Workers to local entry functions while reserving system administration
and account management privileges for Municipal Health Officers.

At the application layer, credential protection utilizes salted
key-stretching algorithms via Flask-Bcrypt to mitigate unauthorized
access attempts. Furthermore, local database files and configuration
keys in edge environments are secured using 256-bit AES encryption via
SQLCipher and Python Fernet to protect against local hardware
compromise. These security mechanisms operate within a broader Data Life
Cycle Management (DLCM) framework, which governs records across six
phases: creation, storage, analytical processing, synchronization,
archiving, and deletion (Oktaviana et al., 2025; Rahul \& Banyal, 2020).

\textbf{2.2.5 Synthesis, Research Gaps, and Proposed Technical Solution}

Despite progress in health informatics, the literature reveals three
distinct technical gaps in municipal surveillance. First, a resolution
mismatch exists because national platforms focus primarily on
macro-level provincial or regional aggregates, leaving municipal health
units without micro-level barangay tracking. Second, existing systems
demonstrate a heavy network dependency, relying on constant cloud
connectivity that fails in intermittent network environments (Kaboré et
al., 2022). Third, an analytics integration gap persists, as most local
health tools operate purely as static storage registries rather than
integrated decision support platforms (Ouédraogo et al., 2023).

This research addresses these limitations through a Web-Based Decision
Support System designed specifically for municipal Dengue surveillance
in Davao City. The resolution mismatch is resolved by ingesting and
mapping multi-year raw surveillance logs (comprising 16,391 cases from
2023 to 2026) through an automated schema normalization pipeline. This
pipeline standardizes variable attribute headers across logging years
into core operational parameters (Morbidity Month, District, Barangay,
Age, Sex, Clinical Classification, Case Classification, and Morbidity
Week) to enable high-resolution spatial and temporal tracking. Network
dependency is mitigated through a local-first SQLite architecture paired
with Python processing pipelines, permitting offline record management,
local querying, and offline analytics generation until network
connectivity enables central database synchronization (Paluyo et al.,
2022). The analytics gap is bridged by embedding a three-tier engine
combining descriptive mapping, predictive 75th percentile moving
regression (Ouédraogo et al., 2023; Thayer et al., 2025), and
prescriptive rule-based alert triggers (Nguyen \& Ming, 2023). Finally,
multi-layered security protocols ensure data confidentiality, system
integrity, and full regulatory compliance with RA 10173 across local and
municipal operational levels.

\paragraph{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{2.3.1 Local-First Software
Architecture Paradigm}
}{2.3.1 Local-First Software Architecture Paradigm }}\label{local-first-software-architecture-paradigm}

Grounding the proposed system's technical design, the Local-First
Software Paradigm (Kleppmann et al., 2019) posits that data-centric
applications should execute core storage and processing functions
directly on client devices without requiring constant cloud
connectivity. In primary care environments characterized by variable
network infrastructure, local-first computing guarantees high
operational availability by enabling offline record management, local
querying, and offline analytics execution directly on edge hardware
(Kleppmann et al., 2019). Empirical evaluations of electronic health
record (EHR) deployments in Philippine primary care settings demonstrate
that system acceptability degrades when software architectures introduce
rigid connectivity or platform constraints across diverse health
facilities (Elepaño et al., 2025). By embedding a local-first SQLite
database paired with asynchronous cloud database synchronization, the
system provides local data ownership and operational continuity for
primary health units during network disruptions while maintaining
central data consolidation capability.

\textbf{2.3.2 Relational Database Theory}

Edgar F. Codd's (1970) foundational relational model provides the
mathematical structure for the proposed system's data management layer.
Relational database theory asserts that data integrity is maintained
through formal normalization rules, primary and foreign key constraints,
and typed attribute fields, which collectively eliminate data redundancy
and entry anomalies.

In healthcare and medical informatics, applying relational schemas
addresses the severe limitations of non-conventional flat-file or
spreadsheet-based tracking, which are prone to record corruption and
duplicate entries (Dilling, 2020; Thomer \& Wickett, 2020). By
structuring epidemiological variables such as Morbidity Week, Morbidity
Month, Barangay, Age, Sex, and Case Classification within a normalized
SQLite schema, the system enforces mathematical integrity constraints at
the point of entry. This guarantees that micro-level case data remain
structurally consistent and fully queryable for longitudinal analysis
(Dilling, 2020).

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{2.3.4 Privacy Calculus Theory}
}{2.3.4 Privacy Calculus Theory }}\label{privacy-calculus-theory}

This theory provides the psychological and decision-making framework for
data sharing in health tracking systems. As established by \textbf{Li et
al. (2023)}, individuals engage in a "calculus" where they weigh the
\textbf{perceived privacy risks} (e.g., data breaches or hardware theft)
against the \textbf{perceived benefits} of public service utility.

This theory justifies the inclusion of robust security measures such as
\textbf{At-Rest Encryption} and \textbf{Credential Hashing} not just as
technical requirements, but as essential tools to mitigate user risk
perception. By reducing the "potential loss expectations" through
visible security protocols, the system increases the user's intention to
provide the granular data necessary for accurate health monitoring.

The conceptual architecture of this investigation utilizes an
Input-Process-Output (IPO) paradigm to operationalize the conversion of
unstandardized community registers into structured municipal
epidemiological intelligence. As illustrated in Figure 3.1, the
framework integrates local-first synchronization and automated
computational pipelines to facilitate high-resolution surveillance
across Davao City's administrative boundaries.

\textbf{Figure 3.1. \emph{Conceptual Framework of the System}}

\textbf{3.1.1 Input Stage}

The system initiation phase centers on the digital ingestion of granular
community health records, originally captured as unstandardized manual
logs. This procedural stage executes primary data digitization, ensuring
that point-of-care entries are normalized and structured for relational
database integration and subsequent computational analysis.

\textbf{3.1.2 Process Stage}

The analytical engine leverages a multi-layered software architecture
engineered to ensure operational fault tolerance, structural integrity,
and longitudinal precision:

Local-First Synchronization Logic: This component prioritizes
client-side record management within a local SQLite architecture,
maintaining continuous system utility in edge deployments lacking stable
network access. Asynchronous protocols facilitate background
synchronization with the municipal database server upon connection
restoration.

Automated Computational Pipeline: A Python-based analytics pipeline
executes the system's processing logic, performing micro-level spatial
spike detection at the barangay level and aggregating multi-year
epidemiological datasets (2023-2026) to generate comprehensive
chronological summaries.

Structured Dataset Repository: This relational schema serves as the
primary data governance layer, managing verified, de-identified patient
attributes within a normalized repository to prevent data anomalies and
ensure longitudinal consistency.

\textbf{3.1.3 Output Stage}

The framework concludes by presenting processed epidemiological
intelligence through a Flask-based web interface. This secure platform
renders interactive analytical dashboards and automated operational
reports, providing municipal health authorities with the high-resolution
evidence required to optimize resource allocation, guide vector
interventions, and refine localized public health policy.

The synthesized technical descriptions provided above maintain strict
alignment with the established nomenclature of the conceptual model,
ensuring clarity for external evaluation. After finalizing this section,

\textbf{3.2 Operational Framework}

The operational framework (see Figure 3.2) delineates the sequential
workflow and procedural stages required to execute the study. While the
conceptual framework defines the system's underlying architecture, the
operational framework serves as a procedural roadmap, mapping the
step-by-step transition from initial health record log ingestion to
local/central database synchronization, analytical execution, and
validated report generation.

\includegraphics[width=6.18403in,height=3.97403in]{./media/image1.png}

\textbf{Figure 3.2. \emph{Operational Framework Of The System}}

\textbf{3.2.1 Phase I: Data Acquisition, Schema Mapping, and Local
Storage}

The initial phase focuses on the digital ingestion and primary archival
of health records:

\begin{itemize}
\item
  Data Ingestion \& Schema Normalization: Raw surveillance files from
  2023--2026 exhibit minor header variations across logging years (e.g.,
  MW vs. Morbidity Week, ClinClass vs. clinical classification, and
  AgeYears vs. Age in Years). The Python ingestion pipeline maps these
  aliases into a unified relational schema containing key operational
  parameters: Morbidity Month, District, Barangay, Age, Sex, Clinical
  Classification, Case Classification, and Morbidity Week (aggregated to
  Morbidity Month for time-series consistency when week markers are
  omitted).
\item
  Local Database Entry: Records are stored locally in SQLite, enabling
  Barangay Health Workers (BHWs) to operate offline during network
  disruptions.
\item
  De-Identification Protocol: To comply with RA 10173 (Data Privacy Act
  of 2012), direct personal identifiers are stripped, and records are
  assigned auto-generated internal case IDs to ensure complete patient
  anonymity.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{3.2.2 Phase II: Network Connectivity \& Synchronization Logic}

This procedural stage governs the evaluation of network states and the
orchestration of cross-tier data routing:

\begin{itemize}
\item
  Connectivity Check: The system continuously monitors network
  availability. Upon detecting active connectivity, local transactions
  are asynchronously synchronized with the Centralized Database. In the
  absence of a stable connection, records are securely retained within
  the Local Database layer.
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
\item
  Offline Operations: Local records remain fully queryable during
  network disruptions, allowing personnel to execute automated reporting
  and localized analytics directly from edge storage regardless of
  internet connectivity.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{3.2.3 Phase III: Processing Pipeline and Web Interface}

\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Tier 1 (Descriptive Analytics):} Maps the spatial and temporal
  distribution of 16,391 total records across Davao City (6,919 in 2023;
  7,714 in 2024; 224 in 2025; and 1,534 in 2026), focusing on Talomo
  District for localized evaluation disaggregated by barangay\_id and
  morbidity week.
\item
  \textbf{Tier 2 (Predictive Analytics):} Executes 75th percentile
  moving regression and historical baseline comparisons to calculate
  severe dengue risk classifications and short-term weekly trend
  thresholds.
\item
  \textbf{Tier 3 (Prescriptive Analytics):} Operationalizes threshold
  outputs into automated, rule-based early warning alert triggers
  (\textbf{Green}, \textbf{Yellow}, \textbf{Red}) to identify high-risk
  barangays and direct targeted vector control interventions.
\item
  \textbf{Seasonal Bias Control:} The pipeline evaluates records across
  full 12-month cycles (2023--2026) to balance monsoon-driven surges
  against endemic baseline conditions using longitudinal rate
  comparisons.
\item
  \textbf{Geographical Bias Control:} Mapping epidemiological attributes
  to barangay\_id (and district boundaries) isolates spatial risk
  factors, distinguishing high-density urban clusters from localized
  environmental hazard zones across Davao City.
\item
  \textbf{Chronological Partitioning:} To mitigate temporal data
  leakage, historical records from 2023--2025 establish the analytical
  baseline, while the 2026 dataset is partitioned for active model
  evaluation and threshold verification.
\item
  \textbf{Web Interface Rendering:} Processed analytical outputs across
  all three tiers are visualized through a Flask-driven web dashboard,
  providing health officers with interactive feeds for micro-level
  spatial and temporal monitoring.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{3.2.4 Phase IV: Automated Output and Reporting}

The final operational stage operationalizes standardized epidemiological
intelligence for municipal decision support:

\begin{itemize}
\item
  Dual-Level Visibility: The platform facilitates granular case-level
  tracking for localized primary care alongside aggregated monthly and
  annual summaries for municipal macro-surveillance.
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
\item
  CSV/PDF Export Generation: Standardized epidemiological summaries and
  trend analytics are compiled into downloadable formats to facilitate
  seamless information transmission to municipal health authorities.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{3.3.1 Dataset Scale and Potential
Data
Characteristics}}{3.3.1 Dataset Scale and Potential Data Characteristics}}\label{dataset-scale-and-potential-data-characteristics}

The proposed decision support system is built on a longitudinal
epidemiological repository of 16,391 de-identified Dengue surveillance
records from Davao City (January 2023--August 2026). To establish
non-parametric statistical thresholds, we use a multi-year historical
baseline (2023--2025; n=14,857), while the subsequent 2026 dataset
(n=1,534) enables out-of-sample evaluation for active surveillance
monitoring.

An evaluation of the raw surveillance registry indicates several
structural sampling attributes and operational constraints:

\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Reporting and Health-Seeking Tendencies:} The primary records
  originate from passive monitoring within public facilities and
  municipal centers. Consequently, asymptomatic or mild cases managed at
  home, as well as patient volumes within private healthcare networks,
  may be underrepresented within the aggregated case counts.
\item
  \textbf{Spatial and Demographic Distribution:} High-density urban
  administrative units, specifically those within the Talomo District,
  demonstrate increased reporting volumes compared to rural or
  peri-urban sectors. This distribution likely reflects proximity to
  municipal health infrastructure rather than isolated transmission
  intensity.
\item
  \textbf{Temporal Patterns and Class Distribution:} Records indicate
  that severe clinical classifications comprise a limited percentage of
  the total case distribution relative to non-severe or suspected
  categories. Furthermore, chronological trends exhibit significant
  variance between dry-season endemic baselines and monsoon-driven surge
  periods.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{3.3.2 Preprocessing, Filtering,
and Data
Structuring}}{3.3.2 Preprocessing, Filtering, and Data Structuring}}\label{preprocessing-filtering-and-data-structuring}

To operationalize the dataset for baseline modeling, a multi-layered
Python processing pipeline executes rigorous data curation and
relational standardization:

\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Header Alias Mapping and Schema Normalization:} The engine
  maps variable column headers across legacy registries (mapping MW to
  Morbidity Week and ClinClass to Clinical Classification). In
  compliance with the Data Privacy Act (RA 10173), direct personal
  identifiers are removed at point-of-ingestion.
\item
  \textbf{Missing Data Handling:} The system utilizes structural
  curation and temporal partitioning rather than synthetic sampling.
  Attributes lacking granular weekly markers are aggregated into
  Morbidity Month summaries for longitudinal tracking but are excluded
  from high-resolution regression to preserve baseline integrity.
\item
  \textbf{Geographical and Seasonal Rate Adjustment:} Spatial case
  attributes are evaluated alongside population density within barangay
  boundaries (barangay\_id) to calculate relative incidence per 10,000
  residents, adjusting for demographic variance across municipal units.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{3.3.3 Model Formulation and
Sensitivity
Management}}{3.3.3 Model Formulation and Sensitivity Management}}\label{model-formulation-and-sensitivity-management}

The predictive module utilizes a non-parametric 75th Percentile Moving
Regression across a multi-year rolling horizon (2023--2025). Threshold
precision and sensitivity are maintained through the following
architectural strategies:

\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Rolling Window Smoothing:} Statistical limits are calculated
  using multi-week temporal horizons to mitigate the influence of
  stochastic, short-term data fluctuations.
\item
  \textbf{Non-Parametric Percentile Calculations:} Applying the 75th
  percentile boundary limits the impact of historical epidemiological
  anomalies that would otherwise inflate standard mean-based thresholds.
\item
  \textbf{Out-of-Sample Evaluation:} The active 2026 dataset remains
  isolated from baseline computation to facilitate model verification on
  unobserved temporal segments.
\end{itemize}

(Note: The exclusion of cases from non-reporting private facilities is
recognized as an inherent limitation of passive surveillance frameworks,
as detailed in the recommendations for future research in Chapter 5).

This case study monitors municipal surveillance across 182 barangays in

\begin{itemize}

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{3.3.5 Mathematical
Assumptions}}{3.3.5 Mathematical Assumptions}}\label{mathematical-assumptions}

The predictive and prescriptive modules operate under three governing
mathematical assumptions:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  \textbf{Assumption of Non-Gaussian Seasonal Distributions:}
\end{enumerate}

\begin{quote}
Dengue transmission trajectories frequently exhibit skewed
distributions. \textbf{Mathematical Assumption:} Threshold calculations
utilize a non-parametric distribution based on percentile boundaries
(P75) rather than parametric Gaussian metrics (μ + σ), ensuring that
historical surge peaks do not disproportionately displace the baseline
midpoint.
\end{quote}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\setcounter{enumi}{1}
\item
  \textbf{Assumption of Spatial Units for Analysis:}
\end{enumerate}

\begin{quote}
Case aggregations (Cw,b) treat each administrative barangay (b) as a
discrete unit during weekly threshold comparison (Tw,b), enabling alert
triggers to reflect localized morbidity reports.
\end{quote}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\setcounter{enumi}{2}
\item
  \textbf{Assumption of Proportional Threshold Scaling:}
\end{enumerate}

\begin{quote}
Tier 3 alert categories utilize fixed multipliers relative to the
calculated threshold (Tw):
\end{quote}

\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Green Alert (Normal State):} Cw is less than 0.75 × Tw,
  indicating routine monitoring status.
\item
  \textbf{Yellow Alert (Warning State):} Cw is between 0.75 × Tw and Tw,
  triggering pre-surge advisories for localized vector control
  preparation.
\item
  \textbf{Red Alert (Surge State):} Cw exceeds Tw, signifying a
  threshold breach for municipal response and targeted intervention.
\item
  \textbf{Purpose:} This provides a standardized computational framework
  for categorizing risk levels across administrative boundaries.
\end{itemize}
